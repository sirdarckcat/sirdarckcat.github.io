# T3: where the all-greedy reach comes from

This is a follow-up to T1 (`README.md`), requested by the research session. T1 found that when the small primes get random
classes, each large prime can strike only a handful of survivors. So where does the all-greedy reach of about 0.72·x·ln²x
come from? All three parts are in `t3.py`.

- **T3a.** The odd primes up to w = x^θ get random nonzero classes. Every prime above w, in increasing order, takes the
  fullest nonzero class of the remaining targets. We measure C*(x, θ) for θ ∈ {0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 0.9} at x = 10⁴ and
  10⁵, with 10 draws each. θ = 0 is the all-greedy rule.
- **T3b.** One all-greedy run whose targets are the odd primes below y = 0.72·x·ln²x. It was run at x = 10⁵ as asked, and
  also at 10⁴ and 10⁶ to see how it moves with x (at 10⁴ this y is 1% past the greedy limit, and 13 targets are
  left). For each prime r we record T (the targets left before r),
  λ = T/(r − 1), the size m of the class r takes, and m_max = E[fullest of r − 1 independent Poisson(λ) cells].
- **T3c.** The same profile for the tempered cover of the primes below 40,111 (167 moduli, taken in increasing order),
  against greedy on the same moduli.

The useful split is not strikes but **excess**. A prime that takes a class of size m strikes m − λ more targets than an
average class would. Over a run the strikes add up to Σλ + Σ(m − λ). The first sum is what classes chosen without looking
would strike along the same path. To finish, the excess must make up for what random classes leave, which is about
0.74·T/ln x. Since T ≈ y/ln y, a reach of order x·ln²x needs a total excess of order x. With about x/ln x primes, that
means an excess of order ln x per prime.

## Reading

1. **Small primes make the strikes, large primes make the difference.** At x = 10⁵ the primes up to 113 make 85% of all
   strikes, with m/λ between 1.00 and 1.05, just like random classes. They make only 3.5% of the excess. The excess is
   7.9% of the targets at x = 10⁵ (6.4% at 10⁶). That is a little more than the 6.4% (5.4%) that random classes at every
   prime would leave. The primes above x^0.8 make 5.2% to 6.4% of the strikes but 55%, 63% and 71% of the excess at
   x = 10⁴, 10⁵ and 10⁶.
2. **The factor ln x is the excess per prime.** It averages 0.454·ln x, 0.458·ln x and 0.458·ln x per prime at x = 10⁴,
   10⁵ and 10⁶. The share carried by the top of the range grows with x. The primes above x^0.9 carry 31%, 37% and 44% of
   the excess. Each of them takes 2.3, 3.0 and 3.9 targets more than λ, which is 0.25, 0.26 and 0.28 times ln x.
3. **Of the three regimes you expected, the first two are there; the top is not capped.**
   - For r up to about 100, m/λ ≈ 1.
   - **The middle** runs from λ ≈ 1,000 down to λ ≈ 1, which is r from about 100 to 2·10⁴ at x = 10⁵.
     - (m − λ)/√λ rises slowly from 1.5 to 5.
     - The excess is smaller than for independent cells: the excess ratio (m − λ)/(m_max − λ) goes from 0.58 to 0.89. So
       the class counts of the remaining targets fluctuate less than independent Poisson counts.
     - For comparison: for all primes below y, the Montgomery–Hooley variance of about λ·ln r/ln y per class would give
       a ratio near √(ln r/ln y), which is 0.54 to 0.78 over this range. The measured ratio is 0.04 to 0.17 above it.
     - So an argument that takes "the fullest class ≈ the fullest of independent cells" overstates this range by about
       that factor.
   - **The top** is where λ < 1, which is r above about 2.5·10⁴ at x = 10⁵.
     - m falls from 5.5 to 1.9 and stays at 0.92 to 0.97 of m_max, at both 10⁵ and 10⁶. The cap does not bind.
     - At y = 0.72·x·ln²x, a class of a prime near x holds about 0.72·ln²x/(ln y − 1) targets: about 6 at x = 10⁵ and 8
       at 10⁶. A class of a smaller r holds more.
     - The handful in T1 is the cap at y = C·x·ln x with C fixed, where a class holds about C targets.
4. **T3a: the reach lost is the excess share of the random range.**
   - Write s(θ) for the share of the all-greedy excess that comes from the primes up to x^θ. Then 1 − s(θ) matches
     C*(θ)/C*(0) within 0.05 in all 13 cases. The measured value is the higher one in 12 of them, so the greedy part
     recovers slightly more than the rule says. See the table "T3a against T3b".
   - C*(θ)/C*(0) for θ = 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 0.9 is 0.99, 0.98, 0.88, 0.58, 0.29 at x = 10⁴ and 1.00, 0.99, 0.92, 0.67,
     0.38 at x = 10⁵.
   - The 10 draws spread by at most 7% at x = 10⁴ and 3% at 10⁵.
   - So a proof that chooses classes only above x^θ keeps about 1 − s(θ) of the reach.
   - s(θ) falls with x at every θ (x = 10⁴, 10⁵, 10⁶): s(0.6) = 0.168, 0.115, 0.077; s(0.8) = 0.446, 0.368, 0.291;
     s(0.9) = 0.694, 0.631, 0.559.
   - At x = 10⁶ the rule predicted C*(θ)/C*(0) ≈ 0.923, 0.709 and 0.441 for θ = 0.6, 0.8 and 0.9. We wrote these
     down from the T3b run before running T3a there. The runs give 0.949, 0.741 and 0.449: again slightly above the
     rule, by 0.026, 0.032 and 0.008. These used 2 draws each, which agree within 0.2%. The all-greedy C*(0) at 10⁶ is
     9.973, which is 0.722·x·ln²x, so this ratio is flat near 0.72 from 10⁴ to 10⁶ (0.712, 0.729, 0.722).
   - The same effect shows in the targets left. When the primes above x^0.9 start, the greedy run has left 0.38, 0.43
     and 0.50 times what random classes would have left (x = 10⁴, 10⁵, 10⁶). Above x^0.8 the ratios are 0.65, 0.71 and
     0.77.
5. **T3c: tempering plans the top.**
   - On the same 167 moduli with targets below 40,111, tempering leaves 0 and greedy leaves 33.
   - Up to r = 233 the two behave alike: m/λ agrees within 3% up to 53 and within 11% up to 233.
   - The tempered vector takes the fullest class at 80% of the moduli. For r between 149 and 997 its classes hold 0.91
     to 0.94 of the fullest. In total it gives up 44 strikes (1%) against taking the fullest class at every step of its
     own path.
   - What it buys is the top. Its 56 largest moduli (617 to 997) take 2.41 targets each, 1.38 times the fullest of
     independent cells at λ = 0.09. Greedy's take 2.02 each (0.96).
   - With the targets below 32,971 the picture is the same: 0 left against 9, and a top bin of 1.26 against 0.95.
   - So the independent-cell maximum, which greedy nearly reaches, is not a ceiling. A planned assignment arranges the
     leftovers so that the last moduli collect more than chance allows.
   - On the greedy figure: the 32,971 you quoted for greedy comes from a greedy that breaks ties at random. Ours breaks
     ties toward the smallest class and leaves 9 targets there. `greedy_cover.txt` has G(1000) = 30,184.
