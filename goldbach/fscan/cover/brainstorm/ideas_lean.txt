SEARCH SPEED (tests)
1. Dedicated squaring in the kernel: cross products once, then double. Up to 1.5 times fewer limb products than a general multiply.
2. 64-bit limbs on the GPU with __umul64hi: 11 limbs instead of 21, about a quarter of the products.
3. Double-precision FMA limbs (52 bits) on cards with fast FP64.
4. One level of Karatsuba at 21 limbs, or 3 blocks of 7.
5. Lazy reduction: drop the conditional subtraction in mont_sqr and keep x < 2n; R = 2^672 against n < 2^665 leaves the room.
6. R mod n costs 672 doublings per test: compute it on the CPU per row and update it in k, or start from a small power and square.
7. Shift by up to 4 bits and reduce once instead of one dbl_mod per set bit.
8. Since n = cq + kM, look once for any exponent structure shared along k for a fixed row. Probably none; one hour to know.
9. A100/H100 instead of L4: 3 to 6 times per card.
10. Spot rentals of consumer cards (4090 class).
11. Volunteers: a WebGPU page or a BOINC application that scans a variant range.
12. FPGA Montgomery farm; 665 bits is small.
13. AVX-512 IFMA kernel, 13 limbs of 52 bits, 8 lanes, for the CPU machines.
14. Product-tree gcd of survivors against primes up to 2^40 on the GPU before any Fermat test.
15. A sieve to 2^40 doubles the prime density among tested rows, so a member dies in half the tests; find the cheapest way to get there.
16. Inside a member, test first the rows that survived the deepest sieve.
17. Tune the number of rows tested per launch by member score.
18. Sieve on one GPU and tests on another so both run flat out.
19. The GPU is at half its test rate: lower the selection threshold until tests saturate.
20. Re-optimise the sieve bound on the GPU by measurement (tests per death against sieve cost).

SIEVE AND SELECTION
21. Sieve to 2^32 on the GPU by one modular inverse per (row, prime): 240 survivors instead of 450.
22. Two-stage selection: shallow sieve, keep the emptiest 20 percent, deep sieve those, keep the emptiest 2 percent, test.
23. A third stage with batch gcd against slices of a primorial.
24. Score by exact posterior: a row that survived a deeper bound weighs more.
25. Sieve deeper only the rows that survive more often.
26. Bit-sliced 16-bit survivor counts with popcount on packed rows.
27. Share sieve tables between neighbouring vectors; they differ in a few rows.
28. 64 variants at once: same M, so the inverse of M modulo each sieve prime is shared.
29. Enumerate only the k in the best classes modulo 443*449*457: structured selection without touching M.
30. Go on: a tree over k mod 463, 467, 479, ... with branch and bound on expected survivors.
31. Sample k by Gibbs/tempering in the residue space of k with weight exp(-theta * survivors) instead of scanning: the cover sampler one level down.
32. Measure the density of states one level down (how many k have at most u survivors) and choose the temperature where sieve cost and test cost balance.
33. Sort all (vector, k) of a batch globally by survivors before testing, not per vector.
34. Drop a vector early if its first million members show a worse survivor distribution than the pool.
35. Reuse a PRP found for one variant in another: rows are not shared across different N0. Dead, recorded.
36. Reuse results between the 122,000 and the 140,000 campaigns: different covers, different N0. Dead, recorded.
37. Bank every member that survives all rows below 100,000 as a semi-success and extend those; it is what sequential testing does inside a member, but check that nothing is thrown away between campaigns.
38. Near-miss mining: members that died on one of their last five rows.
39. Learn a score on the Q = 3001 toy, where successes are frequent: is there signal beyond the survivor count?
40. Reorder rows per vector by which rows kill most often in practice.

STRUCTURE OF N (mostly closed; listed so nobody has to wonder again)
61. N = u^2 + c with rows c + v^2. Closed: sqrt(Q) rows, sqrt(X) members.
62. N = f(u), degree 3 or more. Worse.
63. N - q = a^4 + 4b^4 for chosen rows. One row per identity.
64. Aurifeuillian N. Same.
65. N = c*2^n + d: covering on the exponent, and n < 665 leaves no room.
66. Several rows being perfect powers: forces small bases.
67. A common large factor of several rows: impossible above Q (theorem).
68. Primes in (Q, Q^2) planted for one row each: 12 nats per row.
69. Prime powers and composite moduli: closed (theorem).
70. A row that is a base-2 pseudoprime kills a true success in the search. Astronomically rare at 200 digits; one line in the README.
