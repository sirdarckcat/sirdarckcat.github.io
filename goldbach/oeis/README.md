# The cover depth D(n): notes for an OEIS entry

These notes collect what an OEIS entry for the cover depth would rest on: the definition, the terms, the proofs, and why
the sequence is interesting. They were written on 27 November 2026 by the AI session that coordinated pull request #34,
from the work of the other sessions (see `../COVER_LETTER.md`).

**First, a rule of the OEIS.** The OEIS does not accept submissions written by AI. Its policy page, "Use of AI for OEIS
Submissions is Forbidden" (https://oeis.org/wiki/Use_of_AI_for_OEIS_Submissions_is_Forbidden), says in substance that
every sequence needs a human author who answers for its correctness; that AI must not be used to write the text of
comments or replies to the editors; that an AI tool cannot be named as an author or co-author; and that a program
generated by AI may only be submitted by someone who understands how it works and where it stops working. I could not
open that page from this machine, so this summary comes from search results and should be checked against the page.

So these notes are background for a person to read, check and then write up in their own words. They are not text to
paste into the OEIS form. Section 6 lists what each field of the form needs, as facts rather than as wording.

## 1. The sequence

Write the odd primes in order as r_1 = 3, r_2 = 5, r_3 = 7, r_4 = 11, and so on (so r_k is prime(k+1)). For the first n
of them, choose one remainder c_k for each r_k: any of 0, 1, …, r_k − 1. Call this choice a *vector*. The vector
*strikes* an odd prime q when q leaves remainder c_k on division by r_k for at least one k ≤ n.

> **D(n) is the largest, over all vectors on the first n odd primes, of the least odd prime that the vector does not
> strike.**

Equivalently, D(n) is the least odd prime x such that no vector strikes every odd prime up to and including x. (The best
vector strikes everything below D(n); if some vector also struck D(n), it would miss its first prime later, and the
best vector would not be the best.)

The word "strike" comes from what this does to Goldbach's problem. If an even number N leaves remainder c_k on division
by r_k for every k ≤ n, then for every prime q that the vector strikes, r_k divides N − q, so N − q is not prime once it
is larger than r_k. All the constructions of even numbers with a large least Goldbach summand in this repository
start this way.

The terms for n = 0 to 35 (offset 0) are

    3, 5, 7, 19, 31, 43, 67, 103, 163, 181, 223, 271, 331, 419, 499, 613, 709, 739, 859, 907,
    1051, 1213, 1279, 1381, 1471, 1567, 1669, 1801, 1933, 2137, 2311, 2521, 2647, 2749, 2953, 3079

Files in this folder:
- `b_cover_depth.txt`: the terms as lines "n D(n)", the format of an OEIS b-file.
- `witness_vectors.txt`: for each n, one vector that strikes every odd prime below D(n) and misses D(n).
- `cover_depth.py`: a short program that computes D(n) from scratch for small n and checks the witness vectors.

### The first terms by hand

- **D(0) = 3.** With no remainders chosen, nothing is struck, and the least odd prime is 3.
- **D(1) = 5.** With 3 as the only divisor: the primes 3 and 5 leave different remainders on division by 3 (0 and 2),
  so one remainder strikes at most one of them. Remainder 0 strikes 3, and the best vector first misses 5.
- **D(2) = 7.** The primes 3, 5 and 7 leave three different remainders on division by 3 (0, 2, 1) and also on
  division by 5 (3, 0, 2). So each of the two remainders strikes at most one of the three, and one is always missed.
  Remainders 0 and 0 strike 3 and 5 and miss 7.
- **D(3) = 19.** The divisors are 3, 5 and 7.
  - The vector "2 mod 3, 3 mod 5, 0 mod 7" strikes 5, 11 and 17 (remainder 2 on division by 3), 3 and 13 (remainder 3
    on division by 5) and 7 (remainder 0 on division by 7). So every odd prime below 19 is struck.
  - Could a vector strike 19 too? It would need three remainders to strike the seven primes 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19.
    On division by 3 they fall into the groups {3}, {7, 13, 19} and {5, 11, 17}. On division by 5 and by 7 no
    remainder is shared by more than two of them. So the remainder for 3 must strike three, and the other two must
    strike two each.
  - If it strikes {7, 13, 19}, the primes 3, 5, 11 and 17 are left. They leave four different remainders on division by
    5, so 5 strikes at most one of them, and 7 strikes at most two. That is three, not four.
  - If it strikes {5, 11, 17}, the primes 3, 7, 13 and 19 are left. Division by 5 strikes at most two (3 and 13), and
    they leave four different remainders on division by 7, so 7 strikes at most one. Again three, not four.
  - So no vector strikes every odd prime up to 19, and D(3) = 19.
  - N = 98 has exactly the remainders 2 mod 3, 3 mod 5 and 0 mod 7, which is why its least Goldbach summand is 19:
    98 − 3, 98 − 5, …, 98 − 17 are all divisible by 3, 5 or 7, and 98 − 19 = 79 is prime.

## 2. Why it is interesting

### 2.1 It is the exact reach of the classical way to build large Goldbach gaps

For an even N, let g(N) be its least Goldbach summand: the smallest prime p such that N − p is prime (A002373). To
build N with g(N) large, one chooses N's remainders on division by small primes so that N − q is divisible by one of
them for as many primes q as possible. D(n) says exactly how far this can go with the first n odd primes and no luck.

If a vector strikes every odd prime below D(n), then every even N with those remainders and N > D(n) + r_n has no
Goldbach summand below D(n) (proof in 3.5). Such N exist in every residue class modulo 2·r_1·r_2⋯r_n, so there are
infinitely many of them.

In practice this is where every record search begins. The search for the largest g(N) with N below 10^200, in pull
request #34, used 89 small primes. With those, the best complete covers deal with every prime below 15,907 by
divisibility alone, even with the extra condition that N be below 10^200. The record, g(N) = 160,357, is ten times
larger: it came from covers that leave about 1,250 primes to chance, and from testing tens of trillions of
candidates.

### 2.2 It also bounds the least summand from above, and gives a covering form of Goldbach's conjecture

The same number works in the other direction. For an even N ≥ 6, let k be the number of odd primes up to √N. Then:

- g(N) ≤ D(k), whenever N has a Goldbach summand at all.
- If D(k) ≤ N − 3, then N is a sum of two primes, and one of them is at most D(k).

The reason is that N's own remainders form a vector on the first k odd primes. At the prime q where that vector first
misses, N − q has no odd prime factor up to √N, and that forces N − q to be prime (proof in 3.5). Both statements are
proved in Lean.

So Goldbach's conjecture would follow from the inequality

> D(k) < r_k² for every k ≥ 1, where r_k is the largest of the k divisors.

The known terms satisfy it with a lot of room. The largest ratio D(k)/r_k² among the known terms is 5/9, at k = 1;
at k = 35 it is 3,079/22,801, about 0.14. As a consequence, every even N from 6 to 24,648 is a sum of two primes, one
of them at most 3,079, and the proof needs nothing but the values D(0), …, D(35) and divisibility (proof in 3.5). Using
only D(0) to D(9), which are proved in Lean, Lean proves the same for every even N from 6 to 960.

The inequality is stronger than Goldbach's conjecture, but it states the conjecture in a form where the standard tools
can be measured against it. Standard sieve arguments give D(k) ≤ r_k^(4+ε) for large k, and the Elliott–Halberstam
conjecture would give r_k^(2+ε) (sketch in 3.6). The inequality needs an exponent below 2, which the linear sieve
cannot give even under that conjecture: this is the parity barrier of sieve theory. The computed values, about
0.8·r_k·ln² r_k, sit far below r_k².

### 2.3 It is the Goldbach counterpart of the Jacobsthal function

The Jacobsthal function of the primorials (A048670) asks the same question for consecutive integers instead of primes:
how long a run of consecutive integers one can cover by choosing one remainder for each of the first n primes. That
function drives the constructions of long gaps between consecutive primes, from Westzynthius, Erdős and Rankin to
Ford, Green, Konyagin, Maynard and Tao. D(n) plays the same part for Goldbach: long prime gaps come from covering an
interval of integers, and large Goldbach summands come from covering an initial run of primes.

There are two differences. The targets here are the primes 3, 5, 7, …, which are sparser than the integers and can
therefore be covered much further. And 2 is not among the divisors, because N is even and q is odd, so 2 never divides
N − q.

The best proven lower bounds have a similar shape. For A048670, Ford, Green, Konyagin, Maynard and Tao proved growth of
order at least x·log x·log log log x / log log x, where x is the largest prime used. For D(n), with y the largest
divisor, Lean proves D ≥ y·ln² y / (9000·(ln ln y)²) for all large y (3.6).

### 2.4 How fast it grows

| n | r_n | D(n) | D(n) / (r_n ln r_n) | D(n) / (r_n ln² r_n) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 5 | 1.52 | 1.38 |
| 9 | 29 | 181 | 1.85 | 0.55 |
| 15 | 53 | 613 | 2.91 | 0.73 |
| 19 | 71 | 907 | 3.00 | 0.70 |
| 29 | 113 | 2,137 | 4.00 | 0.85 |
| 35 | 151 | 3,079 | 4.06 | 0.81 |

- **Proved** (Lean): D grows faster than any fixed multiple of r·ln r, and D ≥ y·ln² y/(9000·(ln ln y)²) for all large y,
  where the divisors are the odd primes up to y.
- **Proved** by standard sieve theory, not formalised: D ≤ y^(4+ε) for large y (sketch in 3.6).
- **Observed:** D/(r ln² r) has stayed between 0.70 and 0.85 from n = 15 to n = 35.
- **Heuristic:** order y·ln² y (3.6).
- **No asymptotic formula is known.** Several were proposed while this work was going on and all were withdrawn.

### 2.5 It is hard to compute

Each lower bound is easy to certify: one vector, checked in a second. Each upper bound needs an exhaustive search over
the ways to strike the primes, which grows quickly with n. With only the counting cut of 3.3, the proof that nothing
goes past D(15) took 1.8·10^10 search steps; the refined searches need about 9·10^7 steps at n = 31 and 9·10^9 at
n = 35. The general problem, striking an arbitrary set of numbers with one remainder per divisor, is NP-complete: Lean
proves that the satisfiability problem of logic reduces to it (`Cap/Hardness.lean`). That does not make the case of the primes hard, but it explains why
no shortcut is known.

### 2.6 Where it meets known sequences

- The first four record holders of the least Goldbach summand (A025018: N = 6, 12, 30, 98) have least summands 3, 5, 7
  and 19 (A025019), which are exactly D(0), D(1), D(2) and D(3): the upper bound of 2.2 is attained (proved in Lean,
  `isRecord_and_isDepth`). From N = 220 on, the records stay below it: g(220) = 23 while D(5) = 43. Even numbers below
  the next prime square are few compared with the number of vectors, so the best vector is not met in time.
- A companion sequence would be the number of vectors that reach D(n), counted over all r_1⋯r_n vectors. Two programs
  agree on it for n = 2 to 28: 2, 1, 2, 2, 6, 6, 1, 2, 1, 17, 9, 2, 1, 3, 2, 2, 4, 2, 1, 2, 2, 1, 1, 2, 12, 10, 1
  (`../sublinear/README.md`, section "The same question exactly, for few primes").
- A search of the OEIS for the first twelve terms of D(n) on 24 November 2026 found no match.

## 3. Proofs

### 3.1 Basic properties

- **D(n) is finite.** Given any vector, pick for each r_k a remainder b_k that is neither c_k nor 0, which is possible
  because r_k ≥ 3. By the Chinese remainder theorem the b_k define one residue class modulo r_1⋯r_n, made of numbers
  prime to every r_k. By Dirichlet's theorem that class contains primes, and the vector strikes none of them. So every
  vector misses some prime, and D(n) is the largest of finitely many finite numbers.
- **D(n) is an odd prime**, by definition (Lean: `IsDepth.prime`).
- **D(n) is strictly increasing.** Take a best vector for n, which misses D(n) first, and give the next divisor r_(n+1)
  the remainder of D(n). The new vector strikes everything below D(n) and D(n) itself, so D(n+1) > D(n) (Lean:
  `IsDepth.lt_cons`).
- **D(n) ≥ r_(n+1).** The vector of all zeros strikes exactly the divisors themselves, because a prime q leaves
  remainder 0 on division by r only when q = r. Its first miss is r_(n+1). Equality holds for n = 0, 1 and 2 only
  among the known terms.

### 3.2 The lower bound for each term: one vector

`witness_vectors.txt` gives, for each n from 0 to 35, a vector on exactly the first n odd primes that strikes every odd
prime below D(n) and misses D(n). Checking a vector means checking, for every odd prime q below D(n), that q leaves the
chosen remainder for at least one divisor. `python3 cover_depth.py check witness_vectors.txt` does this for all 36 in
under a second; all pass. Lean also proves these lower bounds for every n up to 35 (`coversBelow_10` to `coversBelow_18`
in `CoverDepthRun.lean`, `coversBelow_19` to `coversBelow_35` in `CoverDepthMore.lean`, and the cases up to 9 inside
`isDepth_0` to `isDepth_9`).

### 3.3 The upper bound for each term: an exhaustive search that misses nothing

To show that no vector strikes every prime in a finite set T (here, the odd primes up to D(n)), a depth-first search
assigns remainders one divisor at a time.

**The branching rule.** At each step, let q be the smallest prime of T not yet struck by the remainders chosen so far.
A vector that strikes all of T strikes q, using some divisor r that has no remainder yet, and then that remainder must
be q mod r. So the search tries, for each such r in turn, the remainder q mod r, and continues. A divisor that is never
used on a branch can keep any remainder; it does not matter.

**Why it misses nothing.** Every vector that strikes all of T agrees with one of the branches at every step, so if every
branch fails, no such vector exists. Formally this is an induction on the number of divisors without a remainder; Lean
proves it (`search_iff` in `CoverDepth.lean`).

**The counting cut.** A divisor r without a remainder can strike at most m_r of the primes still unstruck, where m_r is
the largest number of them that leave a common remainder on division by r. If the sum of the m_r over the free divisors
is smaller than the number of primes still unstruck, the branch cannot succeed and is dropped. The cut loses nothing.

The faster programs add further cuts derived from the same count. If the free divisors' total capacity is only slightly
larger than the number of primes still unstruck, every free divisor must use one of its nearly fullest remainders, so
the others need not be tried; two free divisors whose fullest remainders share a prime strike one prime fewer than their
capacities suggest. Each cut is stated and justified in the header of the program that uses it (`desert_fast5.c` and
`cover_ub.c` in `../sublinear/`), and each can be switched off without changing the answers.

### 3.5 The Goldbach statements, with proofs

**(G1) Large summands from a vector.** Let a vector strike every odd prime below D, and let N be even, with N ≡ c_k
(mod r_k) for every k, and N > D + r_n. Then no prime p < D is a Goldbach summand of N.
*Proof.* N − 2 is even and larger than 2, so it is not prime. For an odd prime p < D, the vector strikes p, so some r_k
divides N − p, and N − p > r_n ≥ r_k, so N − p is not prime. ∎
Lean proves the version with existence: arbitrarily large even N have a least summand, and it is at least D
(`exists_isLeastSummand_ge_depth`, using Dirichlet's theorem).

**(G2) An upper bound for every even N.** Let N ≥ 6 be even and k the number of odd primes up to √N. If D(k) ≤ N − 3,
then N = q + (N − q) with both prime and q ≤ D(k). In any case, if N has a least summand g(N), then g(N) ≤ D(k).
*Proof.* Let c_j be N's remainder on division by r_j, for j ≤ k. By the definition of D(k) this vector misses some odd
prime q ≤ D(k): no r_j divides N − q. Since N is even and q odd, N − q is odd, and 3 ≤ N − q < N. If N − q were
composite, it would have a prime factor at most √(N − q) < √N, odd, hence one of the r_j; that is impossible, so
N − q is prime. For the second statement: if g(N) > D(k), then for every odd prime q ≤ D(k) the number N − q is
composite (it is at least N/2 ≥ 3, because g(N) ≤ N/2), so it has an odd prime factor at most √N, and N's vector
strikes q. Then N's vector strikes every odd prime up to D(k), which the definition of D(k) forbids. ∎
Lean: `least_summand_le_depth` and `exists_isSummand_le_depth` in `CoverDepth.lean`.

**(G3) The covering form of Goldbach's conjecture.** If D(k) < r_k² for every k ≥ 1, then every even N ≥ 6 is a sum of
two primes.
*Proof.* For N = 6 and 8 there is no odd prime up to √N, so k = 0 and D(0) = 3 ≤ N − 3. Otherwise let k be the number of
odd primes up to √N, so r_k² ≤ N, and since r_k² is odd, N ≥ r_k² + 1. D(k) < r_k² means D(k) ≤ r_k² − 2 ≤ N − 3, because
both are odd. Then (G2) applies. ∎
The same argument with the known values: every term up to k = 35 satisfies D(k) ≤ r_k² − 2, so (G2) gives Goldbach,
with a summand at most D(k), for every even N below r_36² = 157² = 24,649. Note that the comparison is with the largest
divisor r_k, not with the next prime r_(k+1): the even number r_k² + 1 already needs D(k) ≤ N − 3.

### 3.6 Growth

**Lower bounds (Lean, `CoverEveryConstant.lean`).** For every real A and every large y there is a vector on the odd primes
up to y that strikes every odd prime below A·y·ln y (`eventually_exists_classes_every_constant`). And for every large y
there is one that strikes every odd prime below y·ln² y/(9000·(ln ln y)²) (`eventually_exists_classes_log`). The idea,
borrowed from Erdős and Rankin's work on prime gaps, is to give a whole block of divisors the remainders of one common
number. A written proof by the consultant session (`../gaps_map/COHERENT_BLOCK.md`), not formalised and read by no one
else, claims a further slowly growing factor.

**Upper bound (standard sieve theory; a sketch, not written out in full).** Take any vector on the odd primes up to z,
and an even N with those remainders. The odd primes q ≤ Y that the vector misses are exactly the primes q ≤ Y for which
N − q has no prime factor up to z. Counting them is a sieve problem of dimension one on the primes, and the
Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem gives the needed distribution of primes in progressions up to moduli near Y^(1/2), uniformly
in the progression. The lower-bound linear sieve then gives a positive count once z ≤ Y^(1/4 − ε) and Y is large. So
every vector misses a prime below z^(4+ε), and D ≤ z^(4+ε). With the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture, moduli up to
Y^(1−ε) are allowed, and the same argument gives z^(2+ε). The inequality of (G3) needs exponent below 2.

**Heuristic.** Treat a vector chosen at random. It misses a given odd prime with probability about the product of
(1 − 1/r) over the divisors r, which is of order 1/ln y. Treating the primes as independent, it strikes every odd
prime below x with probability about exp(−c·x/(ln x·ln y)). There are r_1⋯r_n, about e^y, vectors, so the best of them
should reach roughly where c·x/(ln x·ln y) is about y, that is, x of order y·ln² y. The computed values agree with
that order (D/(r ln² r) between 0.70 and 0.85 from n = 15 on), but nothing like it is proved.

## 4. Checking it yourself

From this folder:

```sh
python3 cover_depth.py check witness_vectors.txt   # the 36 lower bounds, under a second
python3 cover_depth.py search 12                   # D(0) to D(12) from scratch, about two minutes
```

The larger terms, with the performance session's program:

```sh
gcc -O3 -march=native -fopenmp -o cover_ub ../sublinear/cover_ub.c
./cover_ub 35 3079 proof     # finds a vector striking every odd prime below 3,079
./cover_ub 35 3083 proof     # prints NO COVER: no vector strikes every odd prime below 3,083
```

`../sublinear/cover_ub_validate.sh` runs the same pair of checks for a range of n. On 27 November, on four cores, every
n up to 31 took under half a minute each; n = 35 takes about an hour.

The Lean proofs: in `../lean/`, run `lake exe cache get`, then
`lake build GoldbachGaps.CoverDepthRun GoldbachGaps.CoverDepthMore`. `../lean/HANDOVER.md` explains the setup.

## 6. What the OEIS form asks for

Facts for each field. The wording is yours to write.

- **Name.** The definition of section 1 in one sentence. It must make clear that the divisors are the first n odd
  primes, that each gets one residue class, and that a(n) is the largest possible least odd prime outside all classes.
- **Data.** The terms of section 1. All 36 fit in the data field (184 characters); the b-file holds them too.
- **Offset.** 0 (a(0) = 3, with no divisors).
- **Comments.** The facts of 2.1 (the lower-bound construction), 2.2 (g(N) ≤ a(k) and the covering form of Goldbach,
  with the corrected comparison with r_k²), 2.3 (the parallel with A048670) and 2.6 (the first four terms of A025018),
  each with its proof or a reference to it, and the status of the terms from 3.4.
- **Example.** The case a(3) = 19 of section 1, with the vector "2 mod 3, 3 mod 5, 0 mod 7" and N = 98.
- **Links.** The b-file; this folder and the Lean files in the repository.
- **Program.** `cover_depth.py`, only if you understand how it works; the OEIS rule above applies to it.
- **Cross-references.** A002373 and A020481 (least Goldbach summand), A025018 and A025019 (its records), A048670 (the
  Jacobsthal function of the primorials), A065091 (the odd primes). Check each number on oeis.org; this machine could
  not reach the site.
- **Keywords.** nonn, hard, more.
- **Author.** You. The OEIS does not accept an AI tool as an author; the computations can be credited to the programs
  and the repository instead.
- **Before submitting.** Search the OEIS again for the first terms, and decide whether to submit all 36 terms or only
  those confirmed by two independent programs (n ≤ 15), with the rest marked as computed once.
