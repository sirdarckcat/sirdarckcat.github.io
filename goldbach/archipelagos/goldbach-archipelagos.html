Goldbach Archipelagos

Large Goldbach gaps come in groups. In 20 million consecutive even numbers at each of three scales, about nine in ten of the top-1% gaps belong to a cluster of linked neighbours. Call each such number an island and a cluster of them an archipelago. The standard model explains archipelagos to within about 1%. Its score, the number of surviving rows, already uses everything a sieve can see: a gradient-boosted model with 133 features could not beat it.

g(N) is the least prime p with N − p also prime. A number is big when g(N) is in the top 1% of its window (g ≥ 614 near 1018) and huge in the top 0.01% (g ≥ 1,490). The rows of N are the numbers N − q for the primes q below the threshold; N is big exactly when every row is composite.

This page is the evidence layer of the Goldbach Gaps Map, which shows what is proved in Lean and what the research rests on. Why they form

If N is big, every row N − q is composite. The neighbour N + d reuses one of those composites whenever q and q + d are both prime, because (N + d) − (q + d) = N − q. Lags that are multiples of 6 share the most prime pairs. The lag 30 shares the most of all, 67 of the 111 rows near 1018. Beyond d ≈ 614 nothing is shared, and the only link left is that N and N + d agree modulo the small primes dividing d.

The Lean library proves this inheritance (the Needles box on the map) and finds four close pairs among the 303 needles below 109, against 2.7 expected once inherited rows are counted. Here the same mechanism shows up in about 196,000 big numbers per scale.

The model in orange knows exactly this and nothing more. Rows with a prime factor up to 997 are composite, and every other row is prime with one fixed probability (29.8% near 1018), independently of the others. It is the model the 200-digit search runs on.

At the strong lags (R ≥ 3) model and data differ by about 1% on average. A 3–5% excess at lags ≡ 2 (mod 6) looked like a deviation at first. With block-bootstrap error bars (±2 standard errors) it is 1.05 ± 0.09 near 1018 and 1.00 ± 0.07 near 1015, so it is noise. Because big numbers come in archipelagos, counts of them are noisier than independent draws would be.

The control shuffles the big labels among numbers in the same class mod 30030. That keeps every effect of the primes 2 to 13 and removes the shared composites. Two big numbers are linked when they are less than the threshold apart and share at least a quarter of their row values.

What big-gap numbers have in common

Three properties stand out, and all three come from the residues of N.

  • They avoid multiples of small primes. Only 0.9% of big numbers near 1018 are divisible by 3, against a third of all numbers. When r divides N, no row is divisible by r, so r makes no row composite. The same holds, more weakly, for 5, 7, 11 and 13.
  • They prefer quadratic non-residue classes. 57.4% of big numbers are ≡ 2 (mod 3) and 41.7% are ≡ 1. Below 614 there are 57 primes ≡ 2 (mod 3) and 53 ≡ 1, which is Chebyshev's bias. N ≡ 2 (mod 3) makes all 57 of those rows divisible by 3, four more than N ≡ 1 does. Mod 5 and mod 7 show the same tilt toward the non-residues. The Lean session's record table found the same preference among the 39 record holders below 1011.
  • They sit above prime-poor stretches. Each extra prime among the 614 numbers below N multiplies the chance of a big gap by about 0.72.

Does the standard model hold up?

Once the sieve goes deeper than the threshold, it does. Grouping numbers by how many rows survive a sieve to 997, the model's chance of a big gap matches the observed rate across five orders of magnitude. The Lean session's thinning law makes independence an upper bound for moduli above the threshold, and the data sit within the noise of that bound.

The extreme tail

How many numbers in each window reach g ≥ x, observed and predicted (sieve to 65,521). The last row of each scale is the window's record.

With a sieve shallower than the threshold (to 13 or 97) the model expects 2–9% too many big gaps. That has the sign and size of the pair term in the fscan notes in the repository, which already correct for it. Past the threshold the difference is below 1%, and the 200-digit search sieves to 108 against a threshold of about 2·105.

Can machine learning find what the count misses?

Both sides got the same information: the residues of N modulo every prime up to the sieve depth, and no primality of any row. LightGBM saw 133 features. They were which of the 111 rows survive, the survivor count at eight depths, where in the range the survivors sit, N mod 3 to 23, and Hardy–Littlewood pair weights of the survivors. It trained on 2.5 million numbers from the first half of the window and was scored on 2.5 million from the second half, so no archipelago straddles the split.

Gain is held-out log-loss improvement over the calibrated survivor count, in nats per number, with ±2 standard errors from a block bootstrap. Negative means worse than the count.

The survivor count is a sufficient statistic. The theory formula, with nothing fitted, beats every learned model in all three runs. Nothing else in the residues predicts a big gap. The research session reached the same conclusion from aggregate hit counts; this is the direct test.

Even a sieve to 65,521 leaves about three quarters of the uncertainty. What is left is whether the surviving numbers happen to be prime, and only a primality test, or a sieve all the way to √N, can say. At 200 digits the sieve reaches 108 while √N is 10100, so nearly all of it is luck. The outcomes of six neighbours are worth about as much as sieving 65 times deeper, but each outcome costs primality tests of its own.

What this means for the 200-digit search

  • The fleet already ranks members by the best possible score. The count of rows left after the sieve is all the residues can say. Machine learning will not improve the ranking.
  • A cover is a built archipelago, and a much stronger one. It makes most rows composite by choosing N's residues, the same mechanism that tilts the classes above. Natural archipelagos sit on prime gaps, and the largest prime gaps near 10200 are about 16,000 long, against a record of 158,209.
  • Natural archipelagos cannot be added to a cover. N and N + d share rows only when d is below g. Then they agree only modulo primes whose product is below g, which is Lean's shared_row_value. So a neighbour falls out of the cover on more than 80 of the 88 primes. Lean measured the chance that any of 7,000 neighbours of a 200-digit hit is also a hit: 4·10−15.
  • The ceiling behind the 7.43% looks sound. The same model predicts each window's record (g ≥ 3,137 expected 1.2 times near 1018, seen once). Clustering also fades as the level rises: the share of exceedances that start a new cluster grows from 0.10 at the top 1% to 0.50 at the top 0.1% and 0.80 at the top 0.01%.
  • The only lever left is new information. That means a cheaper way to learn whether surviving rows are prime, or a way to find members with few survivors without sieving them all. It is the open problem the research session is working on (goldbach/sublinear/ on its branch).

How this was done

  • Data. 20,000,000 consecutive even N starting at 1012, 1015 and 1018. Every integer of each window was classified by a segmented sieve with all primes up to √N, and its smallest prime factor up to 65,521 was stored. A check against deterministic Miller–Rabin on 1,200 values of g(N) and 12,000 numbers found no mismatch.
  • Model. A row with a prime factor ≤ z is composite. Every other row is prime with probability cz, the share of primes among the z-rough numbers of the window, independently. The model's R(d) is computed from the actual survivor patterns of N and N + d, so it includes every residue effect up to z and all shared rows.
  • Archipelagos. Connected components of big numbers, linked at lags below the threshold that share at least a quarter of the row values. The extremal index uses runs, with exceedances closer than the threshold counted as one cluster.
  • Error bars. Block bootstrap over 20,000 to 50,000 consecutive numbers, because archipelagos make neighbouring outcomes dependent.
  • Files. Scripts and results are in goldbach/archipelagos/ on branch ccr-c6e00296-3g7fp9: gdata.py, theory.py, archipelagos.py, ml.py, budget.py, tail.py, lagse.py, and their .txt and .json outputs.
