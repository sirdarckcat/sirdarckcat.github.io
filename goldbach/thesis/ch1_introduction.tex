\chapter{Introduction: the least Goldbach summand and the three games}
\label{ch:intro}

\begin{chapterabstract}
We define the least Goldbach summand $\g(\N)$, survey what is known about
it empirically and conjecturally, and set up the three record games ---
height, threshold, and budget --- that organise this thesis. We state the
final certified record board, summarise the contributions chapter by
chapter, and fix the standard of evidence that every record claim in the
thesis must meet.
\end{chapterabstract}

\section{Goldbach partitions, and the most lopsided one}

Goldbach's conjecture asserts that every even integer $\N>2$ is a sum of
two primes; it has been verified for all $\N\le4\cdot10^{18}$~\cite{OeS}.
Heuristically one expects not just one representation but many. The
Hardy--Littlewood circle method --- which estimates such counts by
treating the primes as a random set of the appropriate density and then
correcting for divisibility constraints --- predicts~\cite{HL} the
following asymptotic. Define
\[
r(\N)=\#\{p\le\N:\ p\text{ and }\N-p\text{ are prime}\},
\]
which counts the two orders separately except on the diagonal. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:HL}
r(\N)\;\sim\;2\,\Pi_2\,\frac{\N}{(\log \N)^2}\prod_{\substack{p\mid \N\\ p>2}}\frac{p-1}{p-2},
\qquad
\Pi_2=\prod_{p>2}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{(p-1)^2}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
Reading this from left to right: $\N/(\log \N)^2$ is the naive guess,
since a number near $\N$ is prime with probability about $1/\log \N$ (the
prime number theorem) and there are $\N$ choices for $p$; the constant
$\Pi_2\approx0.6601$ --- an infinite product over all odd primes --- is
the standard correction (the twin-prime constant) that accounts for
primes not being truly random modulo small numbers; and the last factor,
a product over the odd primes \emph{dividing} $\N$, inflates the count
when $\N$ is highly composite. The point for us is the heuristic
conclusion: $r(\N)\to\infty$, so a typical large even integer has many
Goldbach partitions. The interesting question is then not merely whether
a representation exists, but how lopsided the most lopsided one must be.

\begin{definition}\label{def:g}
For an even integer $\N\ge4$ admitting a Goldbach representation, the
\emph{least Goldbach summand} is
\[
\g(\N)\;=\;\min\{\,p\ \text{prime}:\N-p\ \text{is prime}\,\}.
\]
\end{definition}

This is exactly the quantity $p(n)$ attached to the \emph{minimal
Goldbach partition} $\N=p+q$ (with $p$ minimal) by Granville, van de Lune
and te Riele~\cite{GLvdtR}, who conjectured that it never grows faster
than a fixed multiple of $(\log\N)^2\log\log\N$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GLR}
\g(\N)\;=\;O\bigl((\log \N)^2\log\log \N\bigr).
\end{equation}
The notation $f=O(g)$ means $|f|\le C\,g$ for some constant $C$ and all
large $\N$; \eqref{eq:GLR} is a \emph{conjectural} ceiling, not a
theorem, and one thing this thesis does is locate it between provable
bounds and measured constructive frontiers.

Empirically $\g$ is tiny and grows very slowly: its record values are
catalogued in the OEIS~\cite{OEIS} (sequence A020481, with records
A025018 and A025019). Exhaustive verification has produced a slowly
climbing ladder --- among its rungs, $\g=1093$ first at
$\N=60{,}119{,}912$; $\g(\N)\le5569$ for all even
$\N\le4\cdot10^{14}$~\cite{Richstein}; Herkommer's tables reach
$\g=8443$ near $1.2\cdot10^{17}$; and the exhaustive-search record is
$\g=9781$, first attained at $\N=3{,}325{,}581{,}707{,}333{,}960{,}528$,
with $\g(\N)\le9781$ for \emph{all} even $\N\le4\cdot10^{18}$~\cite{OeS}.
(We reconfirmed the first and last of these deterministically, both being
below $2^{64}$.) For recent statistical work on the minimal prime in
generalised $(m_1,m_2)$-partitions $n=m_1p+m_2q$, see
Juh\'asz--Bartalos~\cite{JB}, observational up to $10^9$. Strikingly,
all natural records occur at integers of at most $19$ digits. A
literature search conducted for this thesis (July 2026) found no
published \emph{constructive} records beyond the first-generation
constructions of Chapter~\ref{ch:foundations}; every large value of
$\g$ reported below was manufactured, and to our knowledge this thesis's
records are the largest and smallest of their kinds that are explicitly
reported and independently verifiable anywhere. Our question is:

\begin{quote}\itshape
How large can $\g(\N)$ be forced to be, and at what cost in the size of
$\N$?
\end{quote}

\section{The three record games}

There are three natural record questions, and they reward different
constructions. The first is the unbounded \emph{height game}: exhibit as
large a value of $\g(\N)$ as possible, with no limit on the size of
$\N$. An ordinary prime gap is one way to play it, but a strictly
wasteful one: a gap must block every integer offset, while a Goldbach
desert needs to block only \emph{prime} offsets. Chapter~\ref{ch:gaps}
turns that remark into a measured separation.

For comparisons in which size matters, we use two finite games.

\begin{definition}[Threshold and budget games]\label{def:games}
For $Q\ge2$ and $X\ge4$, define
\[
T(Q)=\min\{\,\N:\ \N\text{ even, admitting a Goldbach partition, and }\g(\N)>Q\,\},
\]
\[
R(X)=\max\{\,\g(\N):\ \N\text{ even, admitting a Goldbach partition, and }\N<X\,\}.
\]
The \emph{threshold game} fixes $Q$ and tries to lower the certified
upper bound on $T(Q)$; the \emph{budget game} fixes $X$ and tries to
raise the certified lower bound on $R(X)$. A companion classical
quantity is $S(q)$, the least even $\N$ with $\g(\N)=q$~\cite{GLvdtR};
the threshold function packages many such questions at once, since
$T(Q)=\min_{q>Q,\,q\ \mathrm{prime}}S(q)$.
\end{definition}

The games can share an incumbent and then separate: a smaller integer
just over the threshold improves $T$ but not $R$; a higher summand
anywhere under the ceiling improves $R$ but not $T$. Both happened
repeatedly in the record history of Chapter~\ref{ch:records}, which is
why the two games are kept distinct throughout.

Throughout the thesis, ``digits'' means decimal digits, and a
\emph{nat} is the natural unit of log-scale accounting: a quantity
grows by one nat when it is multiplied by $e\approx2.718$, so one
decimal digit equals $\ln10\approx2.303$ nats. Search costs, size
budgets, and probabilities all live on this one scale, which is what
makes the economics of Chapters~\ref{ch:costmodel}--\ref{ch:closure}
possible to state as exchange rates.

Three reading notes on the board. First, the height record
$\g=1\,157\,341$ exceeds the length of the longest prime gap ever
certified with proven endpoints ($1\,113\,106$, between primes of
$18\,662$ digits~\cite{GapList}), achieved at $2\,480$ digits --- the
construction is not a prime gap in disguise, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:gaps} certifies just how far from one it is. Second,
the threshold record improved from $199$ to $150$ digits in six
certified rungs across two independent pipelines; the ladder, its cost
per rung, and why it stalls near $120$--$135$ digits are the subjects of
Chapters~\ref{ch:records} and~\ref{ch:limits}. Third, the budget-game
value $119\,419$ is attained by a $199$-digit integer and therefore
bounds both $R(10^{199})$ and $R(10^{200})$; a separate $200$-digit
construction with $\g=112\,249$ once led the sub-$10^{200}$ game and is
now dominated. No optimality is asserted anywhere: every row is a
best-known construction, an upper bound for $T$ and a lower bound for
$R$, and Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} estimates how far from the truth each
one sits (spoiler: very far, provably hard to close).

\section{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Foundations and unconditional results}
  (Chapter~\ref{ch:foundations}). Elementary constructions (factorial
  neighbourhoods with Wilson obstructions; primorial deserts) upgraded,
  via Dirichlet's theorem applied twice, to \emph{unconditional}
  unboundedness of $\g$ with explicit partitions; the covering-certificate
  formalism for prime-offset congruence covers, complete and partial; and
  the transfer of the Ford--Green--Konyagin--Maynard--Tao long-gap
  covering~\cite{FGKT,Maynard,FGKMT} into the lower bound
  $\g(\N)\gg\log\N\,\log_2\N\,\log_4\N/\log_3\N$ along even integers
  with exhibited partitions.
\item \textbf{A validated cost model} (Chapter~\ref{ch:costmodel}). The
  expected search cost of a partial-cover construction is $e^{\Ee}$ with
  $\Ee=|U|\cdot\mathrm{boost}/\ln\N$; the model is calibrated to
  $\pm0.1$ nats across five scales and $1.2\cdot10^9$ scanned
  candidates, and it converts record planning from vibes into
  arithmetic: frontier curves $\Ee(D)$ and $\Ee(Q)$, a digit--compute
  exchange rate, two binding regimes, and the boost-versus-kills
  equilibrium that sets cover size.
\item \textbf{The certified records} (Chapters~\ref{ch:records}
  and~\ref{ch:gaps}). Eleven new certified constructions across three games,
  including the $150$-digit threshold record (the first GPU-scale rung),
  the $199$-digit budget record $\g=119\,419$, and two height-game
  constructions ($\g=1\,157\,341$ at $2\,480$ digits;
  $\g=1\,134\,871$ at $2\,692$ digits) that beat the certified
  prime-gap record --- the latter with a fully certified local prime gap
  $126.5$ times shorter than its desert. Plus the engineering that made
  them affordable (measured: sorted testing $1.8\times$, selective
  testing $3\times$ net, a $430\times$ GPU engine) and a verification
  standard that survived adversarial audit.
\item \textbf{Compression barriers} (Chapter~\ref{ch:limits}).
  Joint modulus/representative optimization is capped exactly by search
  entropy (measured to half a digit of its ceiling); set-valued
  conditioning buys $1$--$2.8$ nats, not orders of magnitude, and the
  classic cover is the unique freely-enumerable design; oversized covers
  reduce sub-$100$-digit records to modular subset-sum with choices at
  density $\approx1.026$ and to CRT list decoding beyond the Johnson
  radius --- and practical lattice reduction measurably fails to
  penetrate that radius even on solution-abundant instances. Sub-$100$
  digits is costed at $\sim$$9\cdot10^5$ GPU-years by the only viable
  route.
\item \textbf{The closure theorem} (Chapter~\ref{ch:closure}). Within a
  concrete grammar of planted compositeness-certificate mechanisms,
  congruence covering is optimal: every family is closed by a proof of
  its own kind (value-set ceilings, cluster bounds, isolation, orbit
  accounting, a supply-side purchase argument), swept by $13\,661$
  enumerated and $41$ adversarially evolved instances with zero passes.
  The game's exponent is precisely an integrality gap: the fractional
  cover is perfect, greedy loses $7.6\%$, and exact optimization over
  windows up to half the cover certifies the greedy point locally
  optimal. Two named conditionals (Hardy--Littlewood independence; no
  sub-modexp compositeness detection) and the out-of-grammar frontier
  are stated as open problems.
\item \textbf{Methodology} (throughout; collected in
  Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}). Restart-safe chunked compute with
  git-committed checkpoints; dual-implementation certification; honest
  negative results with in-model statistics; and the referee-hardening
  use of adversarial search --- the falsification engine's first
  discovery was a $50\times$ accounting error in our own evaluator, and
  catching it strengthened the theorem it was built to attack.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Standard of evidence}

Every record claim in this thesis separates and discharges three
obligations:
\begin{itemize}
\item[\emph{(a)}] \emph{No small prime is a summand.} For every prime
  $q<\g(\N)$, the complement $\N-q$ is proved composite by an
  unconditional witness: parity, an explicit divisor (from the
  congruence system or trial division), or a failed strong base-2
  probable-prime test --- a failed test is a rigorous compositeness
  proof, since pseudoprimes can only err in the other direction.
\item[\emph{(b)}] \emph{A Goldbach partition exists.} The partition
  $\N=\g+(\N-\g)$ is exhibited, with $\g$ proven prime directly (it is
  always below $2^{64}$) and $\N-\g$ proven prime by an
  Atkin--Morain/Goldwasser--Kilian elliptic-curve certificate
  \cite{GoldwasserKilian,AtkinMorain}, re-validated by an independent
  implementation, and for the CPU-pipeline records additionally by the
  APR-CL algorithm~\cite{APRCL} and a separate full scan.
\item[\emph{(c)}] \emph{The least summand equals the stated value},
  which is the conjunction of (a) and (b).
\end{itemize}
The witnesses are re-derived from the published construction data by
standalone verifiers --- never trusted from search logs --- and the whole
package is hash-manifested. Chapter~\ref{ch:records} reports the audit
history of this standard, including the two occasions a weaker check
returned a false PASS and was caught by the remaining legs.

\section{Notation}

$\log_k$ is the $k$-fold iterated logarithm (so $\log_2\N=\log\log\N$,
and each extra $\log$ grows \emph{extremely} slowly);
$\theta(x)=\sum_{p\le x}\log p$ is Chebyshev's function;
$\pi(x)$ counts primes up to $x$; $\gamma\approx0.5772$ is Euler's
constant; $f\gg g$ means $f\ge c\,g$ for some constant $c>0$ and all
large arguments (the reverse of $f=O(g)$); ``BPSW'' denotes the
Baillie--PSW probable-prime test~\cite{PSW,BW}, ``SPRP'' a strong
probable-prime test to a stated base, and ``PRP'' a probable prime;
``ECPP'' is elliptic-curve primality proving. An integer is
\emph{$y$-rough} when it has no prime factor $\le y$. $D$ denotes a
size budget in decimal digits and $B=10^{D}$ the corresponding bound;
$Q$ a threshold on the least summand; $M$ a Chinese-remainder modulus;
$U$ a residual (uncovered) set of prime offsets; and $\Ee$ the search
exponent of Chapter~\ref{ch:costmodel}. Named integers recur across
chapters: $\N_{199}$, $\N_{237}$ (the first-generation records of
Chapter~\ref{ch:foundations}), $\N_{150}$ (the threshold record), and
so on, always with their digit count as subscript.
